# Benchmark: System Design (HLD) and Low-Level Design, Groundwork vs the public leaders

Date of work: 2026-10-21. Auditor: independent benchmarker. No repo file was edited. Scripts live in the scratchpad (`measure-sd.ts`, `m2.ts`, `m3.ts`, `m4.ts`, `dump.ts`, `dumpq.ts`).

Labels: **OBSERVED** = I read it or ran a command. **INFERRED** = my conclusion. Competitor figures marked "(fetch)" come from a page read through a summarising fetch tool; I give the URL and treat any "factual error" the tool reported as a lead, not a proven error, unless I say I checked it.

---

## Summary verdict

**Is Groundwork's system design data top notch?** On the chapters I read, yes: it is the most carefully checked and the most arithmetically explicit public material I compared. **Is it better than the big players' data?** Better on depth, accuracy discipline, freshness, and (for LLD) runnable, tested answers. Not better on breadth, brand trust, practice volume, and teaching of the LLD fundamentals. In short: best-in-class _per page_, mid-pack _per catalogue_.

- OBSERVED depth/accuracy: the rate-limiter chapter, URL-shortener chapter, news-feed chapter and Raft chapter each carry worked arithmetic and dry runs that I could re-derive. Three non-obvious external claims I checked independently were all correct: bit.ly answers with 301 and `cache-control: private, max-age=90` (curl, 2026-10-21); the IETF rate-limit header draft is at revision -11, 23 May 2026, still an Internet-Draft (datatracker.ietf.org); etcd 3.6 defaults `--pre-vote` true, election timeout 1000 ms, heartbeat 100 ms (etcd.io docs). 827 related tests pass (vitest, run by me).
- OBSERVED LLD: 26 answers, 363 executable checks, about 136 lines of code each, versus a public leader (awesome-low-level-design, 27.5k stars) whose problem specs are about 270 to 320 words and whose solutions are in six languages but which I found almost no tests for.
- OBSERVED gaps: no AI/LLM system design at all (Educative, Hello Interview and ByteByteGo all have it); at least 6 Alex Xu-class designs missing as full chapters; no teaching of OOP, SOLID, the GoF patterns or LLD concurrency (Hello Interview, awesome-LLD, refactoring.guru all do); LLD code is JavaScript only; most "classic" LLD problems (tic-tac-toe, chess, ATM, library, LRU as an LLD) are absent.
- INFERRED scale: Groundwork is about 100 units (36 chapters, 38 HLD, 26 LLD) against Educative's 209 lessons, ByteByteGo's 30 chapters plus 4 more courses, and Hello Interview's roughly 60 guides, and has a solo anonymous author where competitors have named experts and large audiences.

## Head-to-head samples

Eight pairings. For each: OBSERVED facts first, then verdict (INFERRED).

### 1. Rate limiter (foundational, both sides have it)

Groundwork: `sysdes-rate-limiting` (3,353 prose words, 2 SVG, 5 code blocks, 4 tables, quiz pool, nine sourced links), HLD `microsoft-distributed-rate-limiter` (1,391 words), LLD `atlassian-rate-limiter` (2,136 words, 125 lines, 14 checks).

- Hello Interview (fetch of `/distributed-rate-limiter`): one problem page, up to about 7.3k words including page chrome; covers requirements, placement, 4 algorithms, Redis token bucket with the HMGET plus MULTI race and a Lua fix, fail-closed versus fail-open, about 10 shards, hot keys, dynamic config, level expectations.
- ByteByteGo ch05 (fetch): 5 algorithms, 17 figures, race conditions, Redis, monitoring, hard versus soft limits; no code blocks mentioned.
- What Groundwork has that they lack (OBSERVED in the chapter text): a traced dry run (B=10, r=5/s), the stale-previous-window bug in the sliding-window counter, a ready Lua script that reads Redis `TIME` instead of trusting client clocks, the retry-amplification arithmetic (27x, SRE book's 64x) and retry budgets, Little's-law concurrency limiting, GCRA, IETF header draft status, and a nine-link sources box.
- What they have that Groundwork lacks: Hello Interview's explicit mid/senior/staff expectation split (80/20, 60/40, 40/60 breadth to depth), which Groundwork only mimics with "Say it like this" boxes; ByteByteGo's 17 figures versus Groundwork's 2 in the chapter (the HLD answer adds diagrams).
- Correctness: I found no error in Groundwork's chapter (re-derived 5 logins per 15 minutes = 480 a day; Cloudflare's 0.003% of 400 million is the figure Cloudflare published). Hello Interview's page, per the fetch tool, has internal tension: 100M DAU in requirements and 10M in a deep dive; 10 shards at 100k ops each equals 1M ops/s but each check costs at least 2 Redis ops. The arithmetic point is mine and checkable; the 100M versus 10M point is the tool's reading and I did not re-verify it.
- Verdict: Groundwork ahead on depth and verified detail; on par on structure; behind on diagrams count and level calibration.

### 2. URL shortener (foundational)

Groundwork: `sysdes-walkthrough-simple` (5,096 words, 3 SVG, 6 tables), HLD `google-url-shortener` (1,420 words), drill with 8 or 9 checklist items.

- Hello Interview (fetch, `/bitly`): 8 SVG diagrams, hashing versus counter, 301 versus 302 discussion, 410 for expired, Redis counter batching, level expectations.
- Groundwork has: full collision math (expected collisions N²/2M about 47 million over 5 years, which I re-derived: 1.825e10 squared over 7.04e12 is 4.7e7), the 62^6 versus 62^7 keyspace argument, a four-option redirect cache-header menu, and the click-counter trap with the 694,440 to 72 writes per minute arithmetic. The bit.ly header claim is independently correct (curl, HTTP 301, `cache-control: private, max-age=90`). Hello Interview's page, per the fetch tool, treats 302 as the general default and does not mention the real-world 301 plus short private TTL option.
- Hello Interview possible errors, per the fetch tool (not verified by me): an INCRBY batching description that confuses start and end of batch; row size rounding. I know that INCRBY returns the post-increment value, so the first point is plausible if the page says what the tool says.
- Groundwork weak spot (OBSERVED): `sysdes-walkthrough-simple.ts` line 395 says to shard when "data past a few TB" while the same chapter says 9 TB "is small" and fits on three volumes; an internal inconsistency that the audit already flagged and the file still contains.
- Verdict: ahead on depth and correctness; on par on teaching order.

### 3. News feed (intermediate)

Groundwork: `sysdes-walkthrough-medium` (4,108 words, 4 SVG), HLD `meta-instagram-feed`, `myntra-review-feed`, drill.

- Hello Interview (fetch): chronological feed only, DynamoDB, three deep dives (many follows, many followers, hot posts), no ranking, 4 TB feed storage arithmetic that checks out (200 IDs x 10 B x 2B users).
- Groundwork: 100:1 read ratio math (87,000 reads/s, 870 writes/s), 174,000 feed appends/s, the 17.4 million lookups/s that kills pure pull, "IDs only" feed storage, celebrity hybrid with a stated and caveated assumption (six large accounts followed), and a correct hedge that the hybrid is not universal (cites the Feeding Frenzy SIGMOD 2010 paper). It also separates the CAP label from a product-level eventual-consistency choice.
- Verdict: ahead on depth and epistemic care; Hello Interview is simpler and more beginner-readable.

### 4. Chat / messaging (intermediate)

Groundwork has no chat chapter; it has HLD answers `uber-messenger-chat` (1,282 words) and `apple-imessage-backend`.

- Hello Interview WhatsApp (fetch): about 8.3k words including chrome, bad/good/great ladders for 6 deep dives (connections, multi-device, failure detection, pub/sub gaps, ordering, last seen), 4 functional and 5 non-functional requirements. The tool flagged rounding (200M x 20 / 86,400 = 46k, not "about 40k": I confirmed the arithmetic is 46,296) and an ordering-by-NTP-timestamp claim that is overstated.
- Verdict: **behind**. A 1.3k-word answer cannot match a dedicated 8k-word breakdown, and a chat system is among the most-asked questions. The chapters (`sysdes-time-ordering-ids`, `message-queues-async`) supply the building blocks but there is no end-to-end chat walkthrough.

### 5. Caching (foundational)

Groundwork: `sysdes-caching-fundamentals` (4,238 words, 3 SVG, 2 code blocks, 6 tables), including "hit-rate arithmetic: why 90% is not a good number" and five ways to stop a thundering herd.

- Hello Interview (fetch, `/caching`): about 3.7k words including chrome; cache-aside, write-through, write-behind, read-through, LRU/LFU/FIFO/TTL, stampede, consistency, hot keys, a 5-step interview approach. The tool reported a ring-buffer remark for LRU (non-standard) and a mild internal contradiction on write-through infrastructure; unverified by me.
- Verdict: ahead on depth; roughly on par on interview framing (Hello Interview's 5-step framing is neat and short).

### 6. Sharding and replication (advanced)

Groundwork: `sysdes-sharding-partitioning` (4,057 words; vnodes, hot shards, live re-sharding), `sysdes-replication` (4,755 words; failover safety, leaderless repair, "how four real systems do it").

- Hello Interview sharding (fetch): shard key, range/hash/directory, hot spots, cross-shard, consistent hashing deferred to another page, no resharding mechanics. The tool noted a compound-key suggestion that contradicts the page's own advice and a 4-to-5 shard modulo remap of about 80% described as "almost every record".
- Primer: database section is a bullet-level overview (fetch).
- Verdict: ahead on depth and breadth. Hello Interview has no replication page in the free sidebar I read (the sidebar lists Cassandra and DynamoDB under key technologies, both free).

### 7. Consensus (advanced)

Groundwork: `sysdes-distributed-consensus` (3,417 words, 2 SVG): safety versus liveness, terms and randomised timeouts, a timestamped five-node election dry run, PreVote and CheckQuorum, the leader-term commit rule (Figure 8), read-index and leases, ZooKeeper's different read model.

- Competitors: no dedicated consensus lesson in Hello Interview's free sidebar (ZooKeeper is premium), none in ByteByteGo's 31-chapter ToC, none visible in the Educative or DesignGurus syllabi (DesignGurus lists quorums, leader/follower and heartbeats only). A web search for a ByteByteGo Raft guide returned no ByteByteGo page, so I cannot say whether one exists elsewhere on their site.
- Checked: Raft paper's 150 to 300 ms suggestion, etcd's 1000 ms and 100 ms defaults, and etcd 3.6's `--pre-vote` default true are all consistent with sources I read.
- Verdict: ahead (the competitor interview courses simply do not teach this); "not comparable" against DDIA or MIT 6.824, which I did not read.

### 8. LLD: parking lot, elevator, LRU (foundational to intermediate)

- Parking lot: Groundwork `amazon-parking-lot` has an assumptions block that separates reported requirements from its own, two swappable strategies, an injected clock, 14 checks that I read (fee boundary at 60 and 61 minutes, out-of-service spots, double exit, unknown vehicle type), and a "slips" list that warns against the exact structure awesome-LLD uses (a Singleton lot plus Car/Motorcycle/Truck subclasses). awesome-LLD's `problems/parking-lot.md` is 319 words of requirements and class bullets, six language implementations, and a class-diagram PNG (OBSERVED: raw file and tree). Primer's notebook has constraints and code using an abstract Vehicle hierarchy, no tests in the cells I read.
- Elevator: Groundwork `amazon-elevator-system` (2,237 words) gives a four-state machine, a dispatch strategy, tick-driven determinism, starvation and fire-mode follow-ups. awesome-LLD's spec is 271 words with C++, C#, Java, Python, Go and TypeScript code. Hello Interview has a free Elevator breakdown (not read in full).
- LRU: Groundwork has it as a DSA exercise (`content/practice/dsa-5.ts`, "LRU Cache") and as the core of `phonepe-multilevel-cache` (Strategy for eviction, LRU and LFU), not as a standalone LLD answer. awesome-LLD and Primer both have a standalone LRU.

## Hard truths

1. **Per-page quality is not the same as being "better than the big players".** The big players win on catalogue (Educative 209 lessons, ByteByteGo 30 chapters plus 4 courses), brand and community. Groundwork's area is about 100 units, and the audit's own coverage table shows it matched DDIA, Xu and the Primer only after nine new chapters in October 2026.
2. **Solo author, AI-assisted pipeline, no external expert.** `docs/content-audit/system-design.md` describes agent authors and agent reviewers ("an independent reviewer who had not seen the author's reasoning"). The quality controls are real and the tests pass, but a learner or a recruiter sees no named senior engineer vouching for the content. The author field is "Akshat". Competitors that I scored higher on trust are scored on visible credentials, not on content.
3. **The first-pass chapters contained 65 false or outdated claims and 99 misleading ones across 24 chapters** (the audit, before fixes). Later batches (chapters 25 to 36 and the company answers) went through blind review, but their pre-review defect rates are not published, and I read only a sample. Expect a residual error rate; I found one internal inconsistency in about six chapters read.
4. **The company-question layer is thinner than its headline.** 38 HLD answers average 1,306 words; 30 of 38 rest on one candidate report or on a prep guide; 12 are tagged "guide". "Asked at Google" is therefore a weak claim for many entries, though the site labels it. Competitor claims such as "5,000+ candidate-reported questions" (Hello Interview pricing page) are marketing and unverified, but even at a fraction it is far more than 135.
5. **No real candidate data of its own.** All demand evidence is scraped or linked from public posts (Blind, Roundz, prep guides). Nothing in the repo measures how well learners do on these answers.
6. **LLD in JavaScript only is a real limitation.** The interview languages for LLD are Java, Python and C++; the checks run in the browser only because the language is JS. I did not find a Python or Java runner in the system-design area.
7. **The most-asked designs are partly missing.** Chat, video, proximity and maps are among the most-asked design questions anywhere. They exist only as 1.3k-word answers.
8. **Practice is self-graded.** Drills hide the checklist until asked and then rely on the learner to score themselves. Hello Interview advertises live and AI-assisted mock interviews (pricing page, marketing). There is no feedback loop on an actual whiteboard answer.
9. **Maintenance cost grows with the claims.** Chapters carry dated vendor facts (S3, Kafka 4.2, etcd 3.6, Postgres 18). `npm run sources` is manual and excluded from `npm run check`. Without a schedule, the freshness advantage decays within months.
10. **Claims on the site that the repo does back.** "38 company system design answers with 80 diagrams": confirmed (38 answers, 80 diagrams). "26 machine coding and LLD answers with runnable code": confirmed (26, 363 checks, 61 diagrams). "36 chapters": confirmed. Average length: the brief says about 3,900 words; my prose-only measure is 3,731, the larger figure presumably includes code. No unbacked claim was found in this area.
